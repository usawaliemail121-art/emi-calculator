The EMI Formula Explained

Every reducing-balance loan EMI in India — home, car, personal or education — is computed with the same standard formula. Our calculator uses exactly this:

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ [ (1 + r)n − 1 ]
P — Principal: the loan amount you borrow
r — Monthly interest rate: annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100
n — Tenure in months (number of EMIs)

The formula keeps every instalment identical while the interest-principal split inside each EMI changes over time: early EMIs are mostly interest (charged on a large outstanding balance), and the principal share grows as the balance shrinks. Because of compounding through (1 + r)n, both the rate and the tenure have a powerful effect on your total interest.

Worked example

Suppose you take a personal loan of ₹10,00,000 at 9% p.a. for 5 years.

r = 9 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.0075 per month, and n = 60 months. Then (1.0075)60 ≈ 1.5657, so:

EMI = 10,00,000 × 0.0075 × 1.5657 ÷ 0.5657 ≈ ₹20,758

Total payment = 60 × ₹20,758 ≈ ₹12,45,501, of which interest is about ₹2,45,501 — nearly a quarter of what you borrowed. Check it yourself with the calculator above!

Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers to the questions borrowers ask most.

What is EMI?

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Instalment — the fixed amount you pay to your lender every month until the loan is fully repaid. Each EMI contains two parts: an interest component (charged on the remaining loan balance) and a principal component (the part that actually reduces your debt). The two keep adjusting inside the same EMI — interest dominates early on, principal dominates towards the end — which is why a loan "amortizes" over its tenure.

How is EMI calculated?

Lenders use the standard reducing-balance formula: EMI = P × r × (1+r)n ÷ [(1+r)n − 1], where P is the principal, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100) and n is the number of months. For example, ₹10 lakh at 9% p.a. for 5 years gives an EMI of about ₹20,758. You can see the full derivation in the EMI formula section above.

What is an amortization schedule?

An amortization schedule is a table showing every payment of your loan split into its interest and principal parts, plus the outstanding balance after each payment. It reveals two useful things: how much of each EMI is actually reducing your debt in any month, and how much total interest you will pay over the loan's life. Banks provide a schedule with your sanction letter — compare it with the one this calculator generates to verify your EMI.

How can I reduce my EMI?

You have four main levers. Borrow less — a bigger down payment directly shrinks the EMI. Extend the tenure — spreading the loan over more months lowers each instalment, though you pay more total interest. Negotiate a lower rate — even a 0.5% cut saves thousands over a long home loan; a good credit score (750+) gives you bargaining power. Transfer or refinance — if another lender offers a meaningfully lower rate, a balance transfer can reduce your EMI, though watch processing fees and charges.

What happens if I prepay my loan?

A prepayment goes straight against your principal (after clearing any accrued interest), so every rupee prepaid saves you all the future interest that principal would have attracted. Depending on your lender you can either keep the EMI the same and shorten the tenure (maximum interest saving) or keep the tenure and reduce the EMI (better monthly cash flow). Floating-rate home loans in India cannot legally be charged a prepayment penalty for individuals, but fixed-rate loans and personal or car loans may carry prepayment charges — always check your loan agreement first.

What is the difference between fixed and floating interest rates?

A fixed rate stays the same for the whole (or an initial agreed) tenure, so your EMI is predictable — useful when rates are expected to rise, but usually priced slightly higher. A floating rate moves with a benchmark such as the repo rate, so your EMI or tenure changes when the benchmark does. In India most home loans are floating; when the RBI changes the repo rate, EMIs typically reset within a few months. Use this calculator with a couple of rate scenarios to see how sensitive your total interest is.

What is the difference between flat rate and reducing balance rate?

With a flat rate, interest is calculated on the full original principal for the entire tenure, no matter how much you have repaid — a ₹1 lakh loan at 10% flat for 2 years charges ₹20,000 interest. With a reducing balance method, interest is charged only on the outstanding balance each month, so the same loan costs far less. All mainstream Indian banks and NBFCs quote reducing-balance rates (which is what this calculator computes), but some car and consumer-durable financiers quote flat rates — a 10% flat rate works out to roughly 17–18% reducing balance, so always convert before comparing.

How much of my salary should my EMI be?

A widely used guideline is to keep total EMIs at or below 40–50% of your net monthly income — most Indian lenders themselves apply a similar FOIR (Fixed Obligation to Income Ratio) limit when approving loans, with younger borrowers advised to stay nearer 30–40% to leave room for savings and emergencies. Remember to include all existing EMIs (credit-card EMI conversions included) in that tally. A lower ratio gives you a safety buffer against income shocks and improves your loan eligibility for future borrowing.

Does the EMI change during the loan tenure?

For a fixed-rate loan the EMI normally stays constant. For a floating-rate loan, the EMI changes when your lender revises the rate (typically after a repo-rate change); often the bank instead extends or shortens the tenure while keeping the EMI unchanged — you can usually request one or the other. Separately, under the RBI's external benchmark rules some home loans reset as often as once a quarter. Charges such as insurance premiums added to the loan can also change the EMI at disbursement, which is why you should verify your final sanction letter.
